The Central Question

“Why do dominant political parties begin to resemble one another?”

Not a book about the BJP. A book about what prolonged power does to every political party.

Definition

Congressization

(n.)

— the transformation by which a once-competitive organisation, after prolonged dominance, comes to prioritise the preservation of power over the very qualities that won it power in the first place. It does not mean becoming ideologically like the Congress. It means something narrower and more structural: what Congressizes is behaviour, not belief.

“We no longer have one Congress and one BJP. We have, in every respect that matters to how power actually behaves, two Congress.”

How It Begins

The book opens on the paradox it spends eighteen chapters answering. Here is how it begins.

An extraordinary share of India's electoral politics — from state assembly to Parliament — has become a referendum on one person: Mr. Narendra Modi. He is also not shying away from the challenge of repeatedly proving himself across the country. His promise is a currency that sways people from 'Panchayat level to Parliament'. However, when it comes to delivering on those promises, voters often find that Modi's local branch is either not working or has become too comfortable in the knowledge that Modi will win them elections. Despite the most powerful prime minister promising change, citizens either encounter incremental change or optics in the name of change at the ground level.

There is a temptation to read this as a story about one party, or one personality, and to reach reflexively for the language of authoritarian drift. That reading is available, and the danger it points to is not imaginary. But it misses the more durable problem, which is structural rather than personal. When a single figure becomes the standing referendum of election after election — when the panchayat candidate and the parliamentary candidate both run, in effect, on the same name — something happens to accountability that no mere change of leader would by itself repair. The line of responsibility that is supposed to run from the voter to the representative and back is quietly rerouted. It now runs from the voter to one man, and from that one man to no one in particular.

[...]

We still vote. We vote more fervently than ever. We have simply arranged matters so that the vote, given to one, asks nothing of anyone.
